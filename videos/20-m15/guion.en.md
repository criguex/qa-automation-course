# Script EN · Module 20 · m15 · Mutation testing — kill rate, survivors, Stryker and PIT

Voice: Cristian Guerra, first person, plain conversational English. Reference pace: 150 words per minute. The `[mm:ss]` marks come from the word count of each block.

### [00:00] → E01 · Hook

A small document app. Log in, create, approve, publish. The golden flow is green end to end. And meanwhile, an editor can change their own role to admin, the app answers 200, and that editor approves and publishes alone, with nobody else involved. Every happy-path test was green while that hole was open. The suite wasn't badly written. Nobody had ever asked it about that kind of abuse. A green suite can be excellent or it can be asleep, and from the outside they look exactly the same. Today you learn to tell them apart.

### [00:38] → E02 · Explained like you're 8

Imagine there's a guard at the entrance of your school. Their job is to shout when something odd happens. But a guard who never shouts could be a great guard, because nothing has ever happened, or a guard who's fast asleep. How do you know which one you've got? You make a noise on purpose. You knock over a chair, you bang on the door. If the guard shouts, they're awake: we call that KILLED, the noise got caught. If they don't, they're asleep: SURVIVED, the noise got through and nobody noticed. And you don't hide that. You write it down in a notebook so someone goes and wakes the guard up. In software, the guard is your test suite, the noise is a mutant, a small change made on purpose, and the notebook is the survivor ledger.

### [01:33] → E03 · Theory 1 · Four steps, three verdicts

This module is the thread that runs through the whole course. It's where every technique you've learned gets measured. The war-paradigm motto says it in five words: test the tester, mutate reality. You saw the four steps in module 2; now you take them to a real suite. Step one, generate: build the base reality and confirm the guard accepts it. On the functional track, the base is the golden flow: log in, create, approve, publish. Step two, mutate: change exactly one rule, on purpose. For example, a viewer tries to approve. Step three, audit: run that broken world past the guard, here Playwright against the API. Step four, validate: read the verdict. There are three verdicts, always reported, never hidden. PASS: the unmutated baseline behaves as specified, so the guard isn't crying wolf. KILLED: the guard caught the mutation; the check has teeth. SURVIVED: the guard missed it. That's either a real bug or a blind spot in the tests, and it stays visible, printed in red and tracked, until someone fixes it.

### [02:43] → E04 · Theory 2 · The baseline runs first and stays out of the score

Second block, and the one most people skip. The baseline runs first, and it stays out of the score. If the baseline fails, or resetting the state fails, you don't have dead mutants. You have an invalid experiment. Think about it this way: if login is down, every mutation is going to fail. A viewer trying to approve fails, sure, but because they couldn't even get in, not because the role rule works. Count that as KILLED and your kill rate goes up, and it's a lie. That's why the module's pass criterion is blunt: the baseline runs first, and a 5xx or a failed login comes out as INCONCLUSIVE or as an invalid baseline, never as KILLED. In war-paradigm, the runner does the reset, the login and the baseline before it mutates a single step. If any of that fails, that mutant tells you nothing about the suite. It shows up in the report, separately, but it doesn't count.

### [03:47] → E05 · Theory 3 · Kill rate

Third block: the number. Kill rate equals KILLED divided by KILLED plus SURVIVED. Nothing else goes in. Not the baseline, not infrastructure failures. In war-paradigm's experiment 02 there are seven mutants. Five die and two survive. Five out of seven: 71 percent. On the physical track, the numerical simulations that live in the scientific-qa-evidence repo, there are nine synthetic models: an RC circuit, Newton cooling, chemical kinetics, Snell refraction, and others. Each one has an independent oracle that shares no code with the solver, and a tolerance frozen before anything ran. There are eighty-four paired variants: twenty-seven baseline PASS, fifty-four KILLED and three SURVIVED. So fifty-four of fifty-seven mutants killed, with the baseline kept separate. The three survivors are a deliberately tiny change to the RC model, left visible on purpose. And the classifications came out identical on macOS arm64 and Linux x86_64. A kill rate is only worth something if someone else can reproduce it.

### [04:49] → E06 · Theory 4 · Equivalent mutants, and what coverage can't see

Fourth block. Two mutants that produce exactly the same output are equivalent to each other. You count them separately. On the physical track, two acoustic mutants give identical outputs, so the fifty-four KILLED correspond to fifty-one distinct behaviors. Saying so keeps you from inflating the number. And here's a question you'll get in an interview: if I've got 95 percent line coverage, why do I need mutation testing? Short answer: coverage tells you what ran; mutants tell you what got checked. A line can run on every build without a single assertion that would notice it changed. The mutant changes that line and asks: did anyone notice? If nobody did, coverage was telling you a nice story.

### [05:36] → E07 · Theory 5 · Survivors, the ledger, and mutants as data

Fifth block. A SURVIVED is never deleted or skipped. It's marked as an expected failure, printed in red, and tracked in a ledger until someone fixes it. The ledger is the record of survivors and discrepancies, with the cause, the owner and the next step. That's where the learning comes from. The other key idea: mutants are data, not scripts. Each mutant is an object that changes one thing: a role, a state, a contract field, the order of the flow. One change per mutant, because if you change two things and the mutant dies, you don't know which one killed it. And every run leaves a JSON record: expected and observed status, the git revision, and hashes of the inputs. That's evidence. Finally, tools. To mutate code there's Stryker, for JavaScript and TypeScript, and PIT, for Java. We'll get to them. WAR goes beyond code: it mutates business flows, physical laws or commands.

### [10:53] → E11 · Lab 4 · Stryker and PIT

Now the classic tools, which mutate code instead of flows. Stryker, for JavaScript and TypeScript, is set up with npm init stryker at latest and run with npx stryker run. PIT, for Java with Maven, runs with mvn test-compile org dot pitest colon pitest-maven colon mutationCoverage, and writes the HTML report under target slash pit-reports. Those commands come from each tool's official docs. What's interesting is how they classify. Stryker has more states than our three. Killed: at least one test failed with the mutant active. Survived: every test passed. No coverage: no test covers that mutant, so it survived. Timeout: the tests hung, and that counts as detected. Runtime error and compile error: excluded from the score. Ignored: not tested, and doesn't count against you. Its mutation score is detected over valid, times a hundred. Notice the same idea we saw with the baseline: anything that isn't a signal about your tests, like a compile error, stays out of the math. In the exercise you'll run Stryker, or PIT if you work in Java, on a module with unit tests, and compare its kill rate with what you expected before running it.

### [13:40] → E13 · The challenge and how it's graded

The challenge. One: clone war-paradigm, run the demo and explain in writing why the self-promotion to admin survives. Two: on your framework from module 5, write six mutants as data, one change each, run them and classify them as PASS, KILLED and SURVIVED. Three: run Stryker or PIT on a module with unit tests. The deliverable is the mutant catalog as data, the scoreboard, the kill rate and the survivor ledger. Pass criteria: your suite kills at least five of six mutants, and the survivor is in the ledger with its cause. Each scenario's JSON report has expected and observed status, the git revision and input hashes. The baseline runs first, and a 5xx or a failed login comes out as INCONCLUSIVE or invalid baseline, never as KILLED. And no survivor is deleted, skipped or hidden. Four-level rubric: missing or doesn't run, runs but fails a criterion, meets everything, and outstanding if you also do "Kill it yourself": you fix the rule in app slash server dot js and the self-grant survivor turns into FIXED.

### [14:50] → E14 · Mini quiz

Three questions, with a pause. First: you run six mutants, five KILLED and one SURVIVED, and the baseline passed. What's the kill rate? Answer: 83 percent. Five over five plus one. The baseline doesn't count. Second: the unmutated baseline fails before the mutants run. What do you do? Answer: stop. The run is invalid until the baseline passes. If the baseline fails, the guard is shouting for no reason and any KILLED is fake. Third: you have 95 percent line coverage. What does mutation testing tell you that coverage doesn't? Answer: whether the covered lines have assertions that would catch a change. Coverage says what ran; mutants say what got checked.

### [15:50] → E15 · Wrap-up

Three ideas. One: a green suite can be awake or asleep, and the only way to know is to break something on purpose and see if it shouts. Two: kill rate is KILLED over KILLED plus SURVIVED; the baseline goes first and stays out of the math, and anything that isn't a signal about your tests doesn't count. Three: survivors don't get hidden. They go in the ledger, in red, with their cause, until someone fixes them. Today you saw a known one and a new one.
